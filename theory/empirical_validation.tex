\section{Empirical Evidence}\label{sec:validation}

The experiments test a single claim: under continuous drift, memory must be
regulated by temporal validity rather than by changepoint evidence alone. The
evidence separates layers that are often collapsed: the finite useful-memory
horizon itself, the gap between temporal validity and detector evidence, the
cap-only regime that gap creates, and the predictive cost of horizon
misalignment.

In the experiments, UMR is a backend-agnostic conservative horizon cap driven by
a smoothed local drift proxy. It is evaluated in two roles. First, it acts as a
runtime horizon policy for estimators whose effective memory can be adjusted,
such as capped sliding-window, EWMA, and multi-horizon backends. Second, it is
wrapped around ADWIN as one detector-side instance of the same temporal-validity
rule. Fixed-window methods remain uncapped baselines. The resulting
comparisons are used to make detector-silent staleness visible, not to rank
detectors.

\subsection{Structural U-Curve}\label{sec:validation_law}

We begin with the Gaussian sweep used to validate the finite-memory trade-off.
The setup produces the characteristic U-curve: for small windows, error is
dominated by variance; for large windows, error is dominated by staleness. The
empirical minimum remains finite and moves in the expected direction as drift
strengthens, and EWMA behaves as the natural finite-memory analogue rather than
as a competing theory.

\subsection{Temporal Validity Is Not Changepoint Evidence}\label{sec:validation_delay}

The central empirical contrast is the gap between two questions. The detector
asks whether there is enough statistical evidence of a change. The horizon asks
whether the retained evidence is still temporally valid for the present. Those
questions need not be synchronized.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\safeincludegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{artifacts/figures/cuberoot_adwin/fig_cap_vs_detection_delay}

\subsection{Recovery Asymmetry Under Alternating Drift}\label{sec:validation_instability}

The next benchmark makes local horizon instability visible. The stream
alternates between slow drift, fast bursts, recovery plateaus, and micro-bursts
with irregular durations, so the local oracle horizon need not be globally
stable. In that setting, a single fixed window can look reasonable on average
while remaining locally misaligned for long stretches.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\safeincludegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{artifacts/figures/cuberoot_adwin/fig_horizon_instability}
\caption{Alternating-timescales benchmark. The top panel shows the drift
schedule, the middle panel compares the local oracle horizon with fixed windows
and ADWIN+$\,$UMR, and the bottom panel shows horizon regret over time.
Contraction is easier to trigger under bursts than re-expansion after a
low-drift stretch.}
\label{fig:horizon_instability}
\end{figure}

The benchmark makes two things visible. First, horizon validity can vary even
when aggregate prediction error stays competitive. Second, the adjustment is
asymmetric: contraction of the useful horizon is easier to trigger than
re-expansion after a low-drift stretch. Proposition~\ref{prop:asymmetric_horizon_recovery}
formalizes the same asymmetry in the idealized EMA-to-horizon map. The
transition-window table and the EMA-sensitivity sweep appear in
Appendix~\ref{app:empirical_support}.

\begin{proposition}[Asymmetric Horizon Recovery]\label{prop:asymmetric_horizon_recovery}
Let the drift estimator satisfy the EMA recursion
\[
  \hat\zeta_t \,=\, \alpha\,\Delta_t + q\,\hat\zeta_{t-1},
  \qquad q \coloneqq 1-\alpha \in (0,1),
\]
with $\Delta_t = \delta_{\mathrm{slow}}$ in the slow regime and $\Delta_t = M$
in the burst regime. Assume the horizon map is unclipped on
$[\delta_{\mathrm{slow}}, M]$ so that
\[
  n^*(\delta) \,=\, \left(\frac{C_K}{\delta}\right)^{2/3}
\]
throughout the transition. Write
$n_b \coloneqq n^*(M)$ and $n_s \coloneqq n^*(\delta_{\mathrm{slow}})$.
For any $0 < \varepsilon < n_s - n_b$, define the inverse thresholds
\[
  \delta_b(\varepsilon) \coloneqq C_K\,(n_b + \varepsilon)^{-3/2},
  \qquad
  \delta_s(\varepsilon) \coloneqq C_K\,(n_s - \varepsilon)^{-3/2}.
\]
Then the exact recovery time from the slow regime to the burst regime is
\[
  T_{\mathrm{contract}}(\varepsilon)
  \,=\,
  \left\lceil
  \frac{\log\!\bigl((M-\delta_{\mathrm{slow}})/(M-\delta_b(\varepsilon))\bigr)}{\log(1/q)}
  \right\rceil,
\]
while, after a burst of duration $T_{\mathrm{burst}}$ steps, the exact recovery
time back to the slow regime is
\[
  T_{\mathrm{expand}}(\varepsilon)
  \,=\,
  \left\lceil
  \frac{\log\!\bigl((\hat\zeta_{\mathrm{end}}-\delta_{\mathrm{slow}})/
  (\delta_s(\varepsilon)-\delta_{\mathrm{slow}})\bigr)}{\log(1/q)}
  \right\rceil,
\]
where
\[
  \hat\zeta_{\mathrm{end}} \,=\, M - (M-\delta_{\mathrm{slow}})q^{T_{\mathrm{burst}}}
\]
is the burst-end drift estimate. If
\[
  T_{\mathrm{burst}} \,\geq\, T_0(\varepsilon)
  \;\coloneqq\;
  \left\lceil \frac{\log(1-\rho_\varepsilon)}{\log q} \right\rceil,
  \qquad
  \rho_\varepsilon \coloneqq
  \frac{\delta_s(\varepsilon)-\delta_{\mathrm{slow}}}{M-\delta_b(\varepsilon)},
\]
then $T_{\mathrm{expand}}(\varepsilon) > T_{\mathrm{contract}}(\varepsilon)$.
Moreover, for small $\varepsilon$,
\[
  M-\delta_b(\varepsilon)
  = \tfrac{3}{2} C_K n_b^{-5/2}\,\varepsilon + O(\varepsilon^2),
  \qquad
  \delta_s(\varepsilon)-\delta_{\mathrm{slow}}
  = \tfrac{3}{2} C_K n_s^{-5/2}\,\varepsilon + O(\varepsilon^2),
\]
 so the slow-side threshold distance is smaller by a factor
 $\bigl(\delta_{\mathrm{slow}}/M\bigr)^{5/3}(1+o(1))$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The EMA recursion has the closed form
\[
  \hat\zeta_{t_0+k} = \Delta^* + \bigl(\hat\zeta_{t_0}-\Delta^*\bigr)q^k
\]
whenever the regime is constant with fixed point $\Delta^*$. Hence after the
transition from $\delta_{\mathrm{slow}}$ to $M$,
\[
  \hat\zeta_{t_0+k} = M - (M-\delta_{\mathrm{slow}})q^k,
\]
and after the return to the slow regime,
\[
  \hat\zeta_{t_0+T_{\mathrm{burst}}+k}
  = \delta_{\mathrm{slow}} + (\hat\zeta_{\mathrm{end}}-\delta_{\mathrm{slow}})q^k.
\]

Because $n^*(\delta)$ is monotone decreasing on the unclipped interval and
its inverse is $g(n)=C_K n^{-3/2}$, the condition
$|n^*(\hat\zeta_{t_0+k})-n_b|\leq \varepsilon$ is equivalent to
\(\hat\zeta_{t_0+k} \geq \delta_b(\varepsilon)\), which gives the displayed
formula for $T_{\mathrm{contract}}(\varepsilon)$. Likewise,
$|n^*(\hat\zeta_{t_0+T_{\mathrm{burst}}+k})-n_s|\leq \varepsilon$
is equivalent to
\(\hat\zeta_{t_0+T_{\mathrm{burst}}+k} \leq \delta_s(\varepsilon)\),
which yields $T_{\mathrm{expand}}(\varepsilon)$.

The inequality $T_{\mathrm{expand}}(\varepsilon) > T_{\mathrm{contract}}(\varepsilon)$
holds whenever the burst lasts long enough that
\[
  1-q^{T_{\mathrm{burst}}} \geq \rho_\varepsilon,
\]
since this makes the numerator of $T_{\mathrm{expand}}$ larger than that of
$T_{\mathrm{contract}}$ by the exact threshold relation above. The small-$\varepsilon$
expansions follow by Taylor expansion of $n \mapsto C_K n^{-3/2}$ around
$n_b$ and $n_s$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Effect of clipping]
If clipping at $n_{\min}$ is active near the burst side, then contraction can only
be faster. The observed asymmetry is therefore conservative: clipping weakens the
theoretical barrier in the burst regime, but it cannot eliminate the longer slow-
side recovery. In streams with gradual bursts, the observed ratio can exceed the
step-burst threshold because $\hat\zeta_{\mathrm{end}}$ does not reach $M$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Horizon Misalignment Carries Cost}\label{sec:validation_gap_cost}

The next question is when horizon misalignment becomes operationally expensive.
For each backend, we compare its instantaneous horizon $n_t$ with the local
horizon target $n_t^*$ and plot the excess error $E_t - E_t^\star$ against both
the absolute gap $|n_t - n_t^*|$ and the relative gap $|\log(n_t / n_t^*)|$.
Here $E_t^\star$ is the best error achieved at time $t$ by the fixed-window
oracle grid, and the curve is binned over all seeds and time points.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\safeincludegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{artifacts/figures/cuberoot_adwin/fig_horizon_gap_cost}
\caption{Excess error as a function of horizon misalignment. The relation is
backend dependent rather than universal: fixed-100 stays inside a low-cost band,
whereas fixed-500 and ADWIN pay substantially more once the gap widens.
ADWIN+$\,$UMR sits between these extremes.}
\label{fig:horizon_gap_cost}
\end{figure}

These results expose a measurable tolerance band around the useful-memory scale,
together with backend-specific degradation once that band is exceeded.

\subsection{What the Evidence Shows}\label{sec:validation_summary}

Taken together, the evidence supports five claims. First, the finite-memory
optimum is real and not a fitting artifact. Second, temporal validity and
changepoint evidence are distinct objects. Third, the cap-only regime is
operationally visible. Fourth, horizon recovery is asymmetric under alternating
timescales. Fifth, horizon control has operational value, but its predictive
payoff depends on the backend and on the drift regime.
