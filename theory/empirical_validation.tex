\section{Empirical Evidence}\label{sec:validation}

The experiments make the theory visible in finite samples. They do not compare
named interventions. They read observed tracking behavior against the
useful-memory scale implied by the regime under study. Throughout, the oracle
reference is the horizon predicted by the same variance--staleness law that
drives the theory section.

Four signatures matter. First, horizon sweeps should exhibit a finite optimum.
Second, that optimum should scale differently across roughness classes. Third,
the operating horizon can lose temporal validity before a detector accumulates
enough evidence to signal change. Fourth, excess error should increase once the
operating horizon departs materially from the useful-memory scale.

\subsection{Structural U-Curve}\label{sec:validation_ucurve}

The Gaussian sweep isolates the variance--staleness trade-off directly. Small
windows are variance dominated, large windows are stale, and the optimum lies at
an interior horizon rather than at either boundary.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\safeincludegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{artifacts/figures/gaussian/fig_ucurve}
\caption{Structural U-curve for finite-memory tracking. Left: tracking error as
a function of horizon for several drift strengths. Right: the minimum achievable
error decreases as drift weakens. The optimum remains finite across regimes,
which makes the useful-memory horizon visible before any path-class refinement.}
\label{fig:ucurve}
\end{figure}

This is the basic finite-memory signature. Under stationarity, more memory would
continue to reduce variance. Under drift, the sweep bends upward once temporal
misalignment overtakes the remaining variance gain.

\subsection{Regime-Dependent Scaling Across Roughness Classes}\label{sec:validation_roughness}

The next experiment varies the roughness index directly. For each
Holder-type regime, the construction uses a finite-sample surrogate whose error
obeys the same variance--staleness decomposition as the theory,
\[
\mathcal E(n) \approx \sigma n^{-1/2} + \zeta n^H,
\]
then measures the empirically optimal horizon across a sweep of $\zeta$ values.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\safeincludegraphics[width=0.96\linewidth]{artifacts/figures/roughness_family/fig_roughness_family}
\caption{Roughness-dependent horizon scaling. Left: each roughness class still
produces a finite optimum, but the location of that optimum shifts with $H$.
Right: the empirical optimal horizon follows a distinct log--log scaling law in
each class. The $H=1$ curve recovers the worst-case Lipschitz member, the
$H=1/2$ curve gives the intermediate special case, and smaller $H$ values fall
on the same roughness-indexed family rather than defining separate stories.}
\label{fig:roughness_family}
\end{figure}

The point of this figure is structural rather than cosmetic. If the cube-root
law were the whole story, the right panel would collapse to a single slope. It
does not. The empirical optima separate by roughness class, and the recovered
log--log slopes follow the predicted roughness-indexed family.

\subsection{Detector-Silent Staleness}\label{sec:validation_invalidity_gap}

Temporal validity and changepoint evidence are different clocks. To make that
separation explicit, consider a stream whose local drift rate ramps upward over
time. The useful-memory horizon $n_t^*$ contracts as the ramp steepens, while a
long operating horizon remains fixed. Define
\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{inv}} \coloneqq t_{\mathrm{detect}} - t_{\mathrm{valid}},
\]
where $t_{\mathrm{valid}}$ is the first time the operating horizon persistently
exceeds the useful-memory scale, and $t_{\mathrm{detect}}$ is the first detector
alarm that follows.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\safeincludegraphics[width=0.94\linewidth]{artifacts/figures/invalidity_gap/fig_invalidity_gap}
\caption{Detector-silent staleness. As drift intensifies, the useful-memory
horizon contracts until the operating horizon becomes stale at
$t_{\mathrm{valid}}$; the detector responds only later at
$t_{\mathrm{detect}}$, leaving a strictly positive invalidity gap
$\Delta_{\mathrm{inv}}$. In the lower panel, stale memory already imposes
excess tracking error before the alarm appears.}
\label{fig:invalidity_gap}
\end{figure}

Across detector settings, the measured gap remains positive in every reported
run. The detector-threshold ablation in \cref{tab:invalidity_gap_ablation}
shows mean gaps of roughly $5 \times 10^2$ steps over the tested settings. This
is the observable form of detector-silent staleness: evidence can become
obsolete while standard detection remains silent.

\subsection{Horizon Misalignment Cost}\label{sec:validation_misalignment}

The final question is how sharply performance degrades when the operating
horizon misses the useful-memory scale. Using the same roughness-indexed
surrogate, \cref{fig:horizon_misalignment} plots excess error against the
relative horizon gap $|\log(n / n^*)|$.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\safeincludegraphics[width=0.72\linewidth]{artifacts/figures/roughness_family/fig_horizon_misalignment}
\caption{Cost of horizon misalignment. Excess tracking error is smallest near
the useful-memory scale and rises as the operating horizon moves away from it.
Being too short is noisy, being too long is stale, and the penalty steepens at
class-dependent rates once the operating horizon leaves the low-cost band
around $n^*$.}
\label{fig:horizon_misalignment}
\end{figure}

This is the finite-sample reason the horizon itself matters. The optimum is not
only finite; it is operationally relevant. Errors remain modest in a narrow band
around the useful-memory scale, then increase on either side as variance or
staleness takes over.

\subsection{What the Evidence Shows}\label{sec:validation_summary}

Taken together, the experiments reveal four features that match the theory.
First, finite-memory tracking exhibits a structural U-curve rather than a
boundary optimum. Second, the useful-memory scale is roughness dependent, with
the worst-case Lipschitz cube-root law appearing as the $H=1$ member of a wider
family. Third, temporal validity can fail before changepoint evidence appears,
which produces a measurable invalidity gap. Fourth, missing the useful-memory
scale carries a visible finite-sample cost even when the operating horizon is
only moderately misaligned.
