\section{Empirical Evidence}\label{sec:validation}

The empirical section makes the theoretical picture visible in finite samples.
Its role is twofold: to show the main signatures of the variance--staleness law,
and to calibrate the broader extended and operational regimes beyond the
minimum-kernel result.

Four signatures matter. First, horizon sweeps should exhibit a finite optimum
rather than a boundary solution. Second, that optimum should move with temporal
roughness. Third, carrier inheritance should remain visible beyond the minimum
kernel in extended and operational regimes. Fourth, temporal validity can fail
before changepoint evidence becomes statistically visible.

\subsection{A Visible Finite-Memory Optimum}\label{sec:validation_ucurve}

The Gaussian horizon sweep isolates the variance--staleness trade-off directly.
Small windows are variance dominated, large windows are stale, and the minimum
lies at an interior horizon rather than at either boundary.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\safeincludegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{artifacts/figures/gaussian/fig_ucurve}
\caption{Structural U-curve for finite-memory tracking. Tracking error first
falls with horizon and then rises once staleness overtakes the remaining
variance gain. The optimum remains finite across drift strengths.}
\label{fig:ucurve}
\end{figure}

This is the most basic finite-memory signature. Under stationarity, more memory
would continue to help. Under drift, the same horizon that reduces variance also
ages the evidence.

\subsection{Roughness-Dependent Horizon Scaling}
\label{sec:validation_roughness}

The next experiment varies the roughness index directly. For each
Hölder-type regime, the surrogate error obeys the same structural decomposition
as the theory,
\[
  \mathcal E(n) \approx \sigma n^{-1/2} + \zeta n^H,
\]
and the experiment records the empirically optimal horizon across a sweep of
$\zeta$ values.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\safeincludegraphics[width=0.96\linewidth]{artifacts/figures/roughness_family/fig_roughness_family}
\caption{Roughness-dependent horizon scaling. Each path class still exhibits a
finite optimum, but the location of that optimum changes with $H$. The
worst-case Lipschitz cube-root law appears as the $H=1$ member of a broader
carrier-roughness useful-memory horizon family.}
\label{fig:roughness_family}
\end{figure}

This separation is structural. If the cube-root law were the only story, the
right panel would collapse to a single slope. It does not. The
empirical optima separate by roughness class, matching the path-dependent horizon
logic of \cref{cor:optimized_horizon}.

\subsection{Extended and Operational Carrier Regimes}
\label{sec:validation_carrier_layers}

The next figure summarizes two broader carrier settings: an extended regime based
on low intrinsic dimension, and an operational regime based on fixed-
$\varepsilon$ Sinkhorn geometry.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\safeincludegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{artifacts/figures/conceptual/fig_carrier_layers}
\caption{Extended and operational carrier settings. Left: in the extended regime,
embedded low-intrinsic-dimensional support preserves a carrier near the
$a=1/2$ regime, and triangular slopes stay close to the i.i.d. mixture
benchmark. Right: in the operational regime, fixed-$\varepsilon$ Sinkhorn produces
a carrier whose triangular and i.i.d. slopes remain
qualitatively close across the tested $\varepsilon$ values.}
\label{fig:carrier_layers}
\end{figure}

The left panel concerns bounded-support fixed-span samples with low intrinsic
dimension. In that setting, the triangular-array carrier tracks the i.i.d.
mixture benchmark rather than exhibiting a visibly different exponent.

The right panel concerns fixed-$\varepsilon$ Sinkhorn geometry. It shows that the
corresponding carrier remains stable across the tested
regularization values and can therefore be read against the same abstract
horizon law. Appendix~\ref{app:finite_sample_geometry} reports the corresponding
summary table.

\subsection{Detector-Silent Staleness}\label{sec:validation_invalidity_gap}

Temporal validity and changepoint evidence are different clocks. To make that
separation explicit, consider a stream whose local drift rate ramps upward over
time. The useful-memory horizon $n_t^*$ contracts as the ramp steepens, while a
long operating horizon remains fixed. Define
\[
  \Delta_{\mathrm{inv}} \coloneqq t_{\mathrm{detect}} - t_{\mathrm{valid}},
\]
where $t_{\mathrm{valid}}$ is the first time the operating horizon persistently
exceeds the useful-memory scale, and $t_{\mathrm{detect}}$ is the first detector
alarm that follows.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\safeincludegraphics[width=0.94\linewidth]{artifacts/figures/invalidity_gap/fig_invalidity_gap}
\caption{Detector-silent staleness. As drift intensifies, the useful-memory
horizon contracts until the operating horizon becomes stale at
$t_{\mathrm{valid}}$; the detector responds only later at $t_{\mathrm{detect}}$,
leaving a strictly positive invalidity gap. The lower panel shows excess
tracking error accumulating before the alarm appears.}
\label{fig:invalidity_gap}
\end{figure}

Across the reported runs, the measured gap remains positive. This is the
empirical form of detector-silent staleness: retained evidence can already be
invalid for the present while detector statistics remain below alarm threshold.

\subsection{Horizon Misalignment Cost}\label{sec:validation_misalignment}

The final empirical question is how sharply performance degrades when the
operating horizon misses the useful-memory scale. Using the same roughness-
indexed surrogate, \cref{fig:horizon_misalignment} plots excess error against the
relative horizon gap $|\log(n/n^*)|$.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\safeincludegraphics[width=0.72\linewidth]{artifacts/figures/roughness_family/fig_horizon_misalignment}
\caption{Cost of horizon misalignment. Excess tracking error is smallest near the
useful-memory scale and rises once the operating horizon moves away from it.
Being too short is noisy; being too long is stale.}
\label{fig:horizon_misalignment}
\end{figure}

This is the operational reason the horizon matters. The optimum is not only
finite; it is a scale around which performance is visibly better than on either
side.

\subsection{What the Evidence Shows}\label{sec:validation_summary}

Taken together, the experiments support four claims that match the theory.
Finite-memory tracking exhibits a structural U-curve rather than an ever-growing
memory benefit. The useful-memory scale is roughness dependent, with the
cube-root law appearing as the $H=1$ member of a broader family. Carrier
stability remains visible beyond the minimum kernel in both extended and operational
settings. Temporal validity can also fail before changepoint evidence appears,
producing a measurable invalidity gap.
