\section{Empirical Validation}\label{sec:tgt}

A Gaussian tracker provides an analytically tractable
closed-loop tracking model that instantiates the three roles
$(\Pot,\Act,\Conv)$ and admits
closed-form computation of all Lyapunov functionals.

The experiments verify the component-wise stability template by controlled
ablation of each component under pure drift (\cref{sec:tgt_exp_a}), validate
the estimation error bound of \cref{prop:tci_estimation_error} numerically and
characterize the lag-drift tradeoff (\cref{sec:tgt_exp_b}), and demonstrate
the score as a pre-failure early-warning diagnostic on a real concept-drift
dataset (\cref{sec:tgt_exp_c}).
Code and reproduction instructions are provided in \cref{app:code_repository}.

\subsection{Formalization}\label{sec:tgt_formal}

The Gaussian tracker extends \cref{ex:concrete}.
Let the environment produce observations from a drifting Gaussian:
\[
  s_t \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu^*(t),\, \sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}^2\right),
  \qquad
  \mu^*(t) = \mu_0 + \zeta t + \sigma_w B_t,
\]
where $B_t$ is standard Brownian motion.
The tracker components are:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item $\Pot(S,t) = \mathcal{N}(\hat\mu_t, \hat\sigma_t^2)$: Gaussian belief state.
  \item $\Act_t(\Pot) = \hat\mu_t$: deterministic mean extraction
    ($H(\Act_t \mid \Pot) = 0$, $\delta = 0$).
  \item $\Conv_t$: Kalman measurement update (see \cref{ex:concrete}).
\end{itemize}
All three Lyapunov functionals are available in closed form
(\cref{ex:concrete}), making the tracker a convenient test bed for the
trade-off and diagnostic claims studied here.

\begin{lemma}[The Gaussian Tracker Satisfies the ISS Hypotheses]\label{lem:tgt_iss}
The Gaussian tracker with $\sigma_w > 0$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}} > 0$ satisfies
all four ISS hypotheses (H-ISS-P/A/$\Phi$/Env) of \cref{thm:borromean}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textnormal{(\roman*)}, noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{H-ISS-P}: $V_P(t) = \tfrac{1}{2}W_2(\Pot,\Pot^*)^2
    = \tfrac{1}{2}(\hat\mu_t - \mu^*(t))^2$ is a valid ISS-Lyapunov
    functional; the Kalman update contracts $V_P$ at rate
    $1 - K_t$ per step, where $K_t \in (0,1)$ is the Kalman gain.
  \item \textbf{H-ISS-A}: $V_A = H(\Act_t | \Pot) = 0$ since $\Act_t
    = \hat\mu_t$ is deterministic; H-ISS-A is satisfied trivially.
  \item \textbf{H-ISS-$\Phi$}: the Kalman recursion for $\hat\sigma_t^2$
    converges to the steady-state variance
    $\hat\sigma_\infty^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(\sigma_w^2 +
    \sqrt{\sigma_w^4 + 4\sigma_w^2\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}^2})$,
    a stable fixed point; $V_\Phi = |\hat\sigma_t^2 - \hat\sigma_\infty^2|$
    decays geometrically with rate $1 - K_\infty$.
  \item \textbf{H-ISS-Env}: the environmental drift rate $\zeta$ is
    bounded by assumption; H-ISS-Env holds whenever
    $\zeta < (1 - K_\infty)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Items (i)--(iii) follow directly from the Kalman filter's convergence
properties \citep{sontag1989,MironchenkoP2020}.
Item (iv) is the H-ISS-Env condition of \cref{thm:borromean} applied
to the scalar Gaussian case; the contraction rate equals the
steady-state Kalman gain $K_\infty = \hat\sigma_\infty^2 /
(\hat\sigma_\infty^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}^2)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Experiment A: Component Ablation}\label{sec:tgt_exp_a}

\paragraph{Protocol.}
We run $T = 500$ steps with $\zeta = 0.02$, $\sigma_w = 0.1$,
$\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}} = 1.0$, seed $= 42$, under four conditions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{C\arabic*.}, noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Full model}: standard Gaussian tracker.
  \item \textbf{FM-1} (static $\Pot$): $\hat\mu_t \equiv 0$ for all $t$;
    Kalman update disabled.
  \item \textbf{FM-2} (non-det.\ $\Act$): $\Act_t = \hat\mu_t + \epsilon_t$,
    $\epsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.25)$.
  \item \textbf{FM-3} (absent $\Conv$): Kalman update disabled; no feedback.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Environment:} pure drift with no action feedback ($\alpha = 0$),
so the world drifts independently of the system's actuation.
The effect of action-environment coupling is addressed in the Coercive
Masking Corollary (\cref{cor:coercive}).

\subsection{Experiment B: Sinkhorn Sample Complexity and the Coherence-Delay U-Curve}\label{sec:tgt_exp_b}

\paragraph{Protocol.}
We use the Gaussian tracker at stationary regime ($T = 5000$, 50 seeds, $\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}} = 1$)
and compute $\hat\sigma_P(t)$ using sliding-window estimators with
$n \in \{5, 10, 25, 50, 100\}$.
Mean absolute error (MAE) $|\sigma_P - \hat\sigma_P|$ is measured in the
tail (last 40\% of $T$).

\paragraph{The lag-drift tradeoff and the Coherence-Delay Principle.}
The estimation error has two competing terms
(\cref{def:lag_error}):
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{E}(n)
  \;\leq\;
  \underbrace{C_K \, n^{-1/2}}_{\text{variance term}}
  \;+\;
  \underbrace{\zeta \cdot \tfrac{n}{2}}_{\text{lag-bias term}}.
\end{equation}
At large $n$ or fast drift, the lag-bias term dominates and the total
error increases with $n$, hiding the $O(n^{-1/2})$ variance rate.

To validate \cref{prop:coherence_delay} empirically, we additionally run
a U-curve sweep: for each of four drift rates
$\zeta \in \{0.001, 0.005, 0.01, 0.05\}$, we measure the raw tracking
error $|\mu^*(t) - \bar{s}_{t-n:t}|$ across
$n \in \{5, 10, 20, 50, 75, 100, 150, 200, 300, 500\}$
in a passive environment (influence $= 0$) over $T = 6{,}000$ steps and
20 seeds. For each drift level we extract the empirical minimum over the window
grid and then fit the log--log slope of $\mathcal{E}_{\min}$ against $\zeta$ by
ordinary least squares.

\paragraph{Computational budget.}
While evaluating exact Wasserstein distances scales poorly, the debiased Sinkhorn
divergences used for $\hat\sigma_P$ and $\hat\sigma_A$ are highly parallelizable.
\Cref{fig:gaussian_sinkhorn_runtime} and \cref{tab:sinkhorn_runtime} profile the
computational cost across sequence lengths $n \in \{25, 50, 100\}$, ambient
dimensions $d \in \{2, 8, 32, 64, 128, 256\}$, and regularization parameters
$\varepsilon \in \{0.02, 0.05, 0.2, 1.0\}$. The metrics isolate the pairwise
distance matrix computation and Sinkhorn iteration loop. On this grid the solver
remains stable as dimension increases, but fixed $\varepsilon$ is not portable:
larger regularization is consistently cheaper and also gives lower absolute bias
on the Gaussian clouds we tested. For instance, at $d=8$ and $n=100$, mean solve
time falls from 7.341\,ms at $\varepsilon=0.05$ to 0.661\,ms at $\varepsilon=1.0$,
while mean absolute bias falls from 0.6102 to 0.1383; at $d=256$ and $n=100$,
the corresponding pair is 0.799\,ms / 1.7778 at $\varepsilon=0.05$ versus
0.571\,ms / 0.1446 at $\varepsilon=1.0$. The practical message is that
intrinsic-dimension-aware calibration matters even when the same $S_\varepsilon$
machinery is used throughout.

\subsection{Experiment C: ELEC2 Early-Warning Lead Time}\label{sec:tgt_exp_c}

\paragraph{Dataset.}
The ELEC2 dataset \citep{Harries1999} records 45,312 hourly observations of
electricity demand in New South Wales, Australia, exhibiting documented
concept drift due to market deregulation.
We apply a 1D Gaussian tracker to the normalized demand stream.

\paragraph{Protocol.}
Failures are detected by the Page-Hinkley test
\citep[threshold $= 84$, $\delta = 0.01$]{Harries1999},
yielding 113 regime-shift events on the normalized NSW demand stream.
$\ScoreHat$ is computed from a 1D Gaussian tracker on the normalized target
stream, with all detector hyperparameters fixed before evaluation. The passive
benchmark therefore uses no effort proxy and no adaptive threshold retuning on
future events.
$\mathrm{CUSUM}$, forgetting-factor RLS, and scalar Kalman baselines are run on
the same normalized stream, while ADWIN consumes the residual stream used in
the original benchmark.
The Fr\'echet baseline compares each current window to the healthy prefix in the
same scalar representation space.
$\ScoreHat$ warnings are recorded on each falling-edge crossing of
$\hat\sigma_P < 0.295$.
Because ELEC2 exposes no direct actuation or effort channel, this benchmark
uses the estimable score rather than $\ScoreE$; the effort-corrected
score is reserved for the active/coercive setting of \cref{sec:particle_tracking_masking}.
Lead time is defined as the gap between a warning and the nearest subsequent
Page-Hinkley event, using an adaptive maximum gap set to half the median
inter-event interval (nearest-future matching; see \cref{app:code_repository} for implementation).

\paragraph{Results.}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\safeincludegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_elec2}
\caption{ELEC2 early-warning diagnostic.
  \emph{Top:} the Coherence Index estimate $\widehat{CI}$ via $\hat\sigma_P(t)$ (sliding window $n=100$) with
  warning threshold $0.295$ (dashed red).
  Vertical lines mark CI warnings (red), ADWIN warnings (purple), and
  Page-Hinkley failures (grey). \emph{Bottom:} residual signal fed to

Across the 45,312-step stream, the fixed-$n=100$ score issues 58 warnings
(falling-edge crossings of $\hat\sigma_P < 0.295$), of which
\textbf{18 are matched to a subsequent Page-Hinkley event} within the
adaptive matching gap (half the median inter-event interval; 166 steps here).
The median lead time is \textbf{94 steps}; mean is 96.9; minimum 45;
maximum 157.
For comparison, ADWIN with $\delta = 0.03$ on the same residual stream issues
114 warnings, of which 54 are matched to a subsequent Page-Hinkley event,
at 47.4\% precision and median lead 84.5.
At lower thresholds the detector becomes more conservative; at higher
thresholds it produces more warnings but the same one-to-one matching rule
reduces effective precision.
On this passive benchmark ADWIN remains
the strongest generic change detector under this calibration, while the score
continues to serve as a tuneable structural diagnostic whose score semantics
carry over to the active setting where $\ScoreE$ becomes the correct
observable.

\subsection{Experiment D: Dynamic \texorpdfstring{$n^*_t$}{n-star-t} Optimization on ELEC2}\label{sec:tgt_exp_d}

\Cref{prop:coherence_delay} provides the self-tuning rule
$n^*_t = (C_K/\hat\zeta_t)^{2/3}$.
This experiment tests whether deploying that rule dynamically on ELEC2
improves on the fixed-$n=100$ score baseline while remaining interpretable next
to a strong passive detector.

\paragraph{Protocol.}
We estimate the local drift rate at each step using an
exponentially weighted moving average (EMA, decay $\alpha = 0.05$)
of the absolute change in mean between consecutive windows of $d = 50$ steps:
$\hat\zeta_t = \text{EMA}_\alpha(|{\bar{s}}_{t-d:t} - {\bar{s}}_{t-2d:t-d}|/d)$.
The constant $C_K$ is calibrated once on the first 2,000 steps
(near-stationary): $C_K \approx 1.25\,\overline{|s_t - \bar{s}|} \cdot \sqrt{100}$.
The resulting $n^*_t = (C_K/\hat\zeta_t)^{2/3}$ is clipped to $[30, 300]$,
with $\hat\zeta_t$ estimated from consecutive 24-step means and EMA decay
$\alpha = 0.03$.
The full score sweep in the figure includes fixed $n = 50$, $100$, and $300$ as
references. The main quantitative comparison reported in the table uses the
validated fixed-$n=100$ baseline, the dynamic $n^*_t$ rule, and ADWIN on the
same residual stream as an external passive baseline.
Warnings and lead times are computed identically to \cref{sec:tgt_exp_c},
using the same warning threshold $0.295$ and one-to-one future matching.

The results are qualitatively consistent with \cref{prop:coherence_delay}, but
the passive-stream nature of ELEC2 keeps the advantage modest. Relative to the
validated fixed-$n=100$ baseline, the dynamic rule increases matched leads from
18 to 29 and slightly improves median lead time from 94 to 101, but both remain
well behind ADWIN on raw passive detection.

The added passive baselines do not change that ordering. CUSUM is highly
sensitive but imprecise; FF-RLS and Kalman sit between the fixed-window score
and ADWIN, while the Fr\'echet baseline is too conservative on ELEC2 to fire at
this calibration. The kernel MMD baseline (Gaussian RBF, median-heuristic
bandwidth, window vs.\ prefix comparison) achieves 48 matched leads at
37\% precision, placing it between ADWIN and the score-based diagnostics.
None overtakes ADWIN on matched leads or precision. This is
expected: ADWIN is designed for passive changepoint detection, whereas the score
targets structural validity under feedback and extends naturally to the
effort-corrected $\ScoreE$ used in the active masking experiment. The Bikes
benchmark below shows the same passive-stream pattern on a longer real-world
demand sequence.

The trade-off is structural and expected: the dynamic rule improves on the
static score baseline without changing the basic passive-stream ordering, which
is exactly the honest reading needed for the later active benchmarks.

The dynamic rule is also moderately robust to calibration error, as predicted by
the $2/3$ sensitivity of $n_t^*$. On ELEC2, scaling $\hat\zeta_t$ by
$0.75\times$, $1.5\times$, and $2.0\times$ moves the mean dynamic window from
$47.7$ to $57.8$, $36.7$, and $31.9$, while matched leads change from $29$ to
$22$, $21$, and $19$. Perturbing $C_K$ by the same factors gives mean windows
$39.5$, $62.5$, and $75.7$ with $25$, $18$, and $19$ matched leads. The Bikes
benchmark is more tolerant: $\hat\zeta_t$ perturbations yield $92$, $76$, and
$74$ matched leads, while scaling $C_K$ upward to $1.5\times$ and $2.0\times$
actually increases matched leads to $98$ and $110$. These shifts are material
but not catastrophic, which is the practical point of the sublinear sensitivity
law.

\subsection{Experiment E: Flagship Real-Data Masking on KuaiRand}\label{sec:tgt_exp_e}

\paragraph{Dataset and protocol.}
We treat KuaiRand-Pure as the real-data masking benchmark. The
random-exposure slice is treated as healthy, the first half of the later
standard-recommendation slice as bubble formation, and the second half as
collapse. The benchmark is evaluated on 367 users with at least 20
interactions in each relevant phase. User signals are computed on 20-step
windows over \texttt{watch\_ratio} with video-tag exposure summaries; the
effort term is the KL divergence between the current exposure-tag distribution
and the user's healthy random-exposure baseline. The KuaiRand implementation
uses a surrogate $\sigma_A$ based on normalized exposure entropy rather than on
replicated action logs, so the benchmark should be read as an operational
logged-data proxy for actuation reproducibility rather than as exact
$e^{-H(\Act\mid\Pot)}$. To keep the comparison honest, the score and
$\ScoreE$ thresholds are calibrated only on healthy random-exposure windows,
using the 20th percentile of the healthy score distribution, and the reported
$E_0$ is the median healthy effort scale. We also report an EWMA-smoothed
$\ScoreE$ baseline to distinguish genuine masking from simple temporal
smoothing. The main metric is a user-level detection rate: a user counts as
detected for bubble or collapse if the corresponding warning fires within a
fixed four-window neighborhood before the annotated phase boundary, and the
reported uncertainty intervals are paired bootstrap intervals over users. The
companion protocol appendix also records shuffled-label, random-boundary, and
weak off-policy sanity controls; these are reported as safeguards against
trivial exposure-shift explanations, not as causal identification.

\subsection{Experiment F: Feedback Masking in the Particle Tracker}\label{sec:particle_tracking_masking}

This experiment instantiates \cref{cor:coercive,def:tci_effort} in the
non-Gaussian, non-linear particle tracker. The environment evolves according to the scalar
particle-filter simulator implemented in \texttt{experiments/particle/model.py}, but now
with an explicit action-coupling parameter $\alpha \in [0,1]$ so that the
actuation can pull the latent state toward the stale controller output. In
the synthetic setting we operationalise the effort proxy as the \emph{realized
coercive effort}
\[
  E_t^{\mathrm{sim}} = \alpha\,|a_t - s_t^{\mathrm{free}}|,
\]
where $s_t^{\mathrm{free}}$ is the latent state that would have arisen at
time $t$ before applying control. This distinguishes mere action-world
mismatch from actual world-forcing.

\paragraph{Protocol.}
We freeze $\Pot$ (FM-1) and compare two regimes: passive ($\alpha = 0$)
and coercive ($\alpha > 0$). The main single-run diagnostic uses
$\alpha = 0.3$ and effort penalty $\lambda = 3.0$. We then sweep
$\alpha \in \{0.1,0.2,0.3,0.5,0.7,0.9\}$ and
$\lambda \in \{0.5,1.0,2.0,3.0,4.0\}$ over 8 seeds, exporting all raw and
aggregated metrics from the experiment harness. To keep the main text readable,
\cref{tab:particle_tracking_masking} reports a five-regime masking matrix at
the default $\lambda = 3.0$: passive, weak, mild, moderate, and strong
coercive coupling.

\subsection{Passive and Active Diagnostics Are Complementary}\label{sec:tgt_complementary}

The experiments support a clean division of labor. On passive streams such as
ELEC2 and Bikes, the relevant anomaly is changepoint detection on exogenous
data. In that regime, generic detectors such as ADWIN are the right default
tools, and the score should be read as a structural diagnostic with tunable
semantics rather than as a universal replacement for passive alarms.

On active or logged benchmarks, the target anomaly changes. In the particle
tracker and KuaiRand, the problem is not only that the data distribution moves,
but that intervention can keep apparent model-world fit high while a stale model
is being protected by effort. In that regime, $\ScoreE$ and the masking gap are
the relevant observables. KSWIN remains a competitive generic detector on
KuaiRand, but it pays with false positives comparable to or higher than the
effort-aware diagnostic, while ADWIN and PageHinkley contribute little under the
same calibration. The practical conclusion is therefore complementary rather
than adversarial: use passive drift detectors for passive streams, and use
effort-aware coherence when the system can actively shape the observations it is
judged against.

\subsection{Discussion: Limitations of the Empirical Validation}\label{sec:tgt_discussion}

Four limitations bound the scope of these results.
First, the Gaussian-linear setting satisfies H-ISS-P/A/$\Phi$/Env by
construction (\cref{ex:concrete}); non-Gaussian, non-linear systems require
case-by-case hypothesis verification.
Second, the Sinkhorn rate is validated only for the finite-memory,
sliding-window estimator class analyzed here, and only in the
variance-dominated regime ($\zeta \leq 0.001$, $n \leq 100$);
high-dimensional or fast-drift systems require the debiased $S_\varepsilon$
with lag correction.
Third, the ELEC2 failure definition (Page-Hinkley events) is one operational
choice; different failure definitions yield different lead-time statistics.
Fourth, the dynamic $n^*_t$ strategy's precision depends critically on
$\hat\zeta_t$ estimation quality; a more accurate real-time drift estimator
would sharpen the precision-recall frontier.

The Gaussian tracker provides the reproducible, analytically tractable validation
of the trade-off and diagnostic claims.
High-dimensional, non-Gaussian, and non-linear extensions constitute
open directions for future validation (\cref{sec:open}).
