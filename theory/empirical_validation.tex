\section{Empirical Validation}\label{sec:validation}

The experiments test a single claim: under continuous drift, memory must be
regulated by temporal validity rather than by statistical evidence of abrupt
change alone. Empirically, the paper separates three layers that are often
collapsed: horizon geometry, horizon tracking, and predictive payoff. The
Gaussian sweep and lag--variance frontier validate geometry. The
continuous-drift, temporal-validity, and alternating-timescales benchmarks probe
tracking. The horizon cost curve assesses predictive payoff. A compact piecewise
benchmark is retained only as a phase-wise calibration check.

\subsection{Law Validation}\label{sec:validation_law}

We begin with the Gaussian sweep used to validate the finite-memory law. The
setup produces the characteristic U-curve: for small windows, error is dominated
by variance; for large windows, error is dominated by staleness. The empirical
minimum follows the predicted cube-root scaling, and EWMA behaves as the natural
finite-memory analogue rather than as a competing theory.

methods on a smooth drifting stream: a short fixed window, a long fixed window,

becomes stale before changepoint evidence accumulates.

\subsection{Fixed-Horizon Instability}\label{sec:validation_instability}

The next benchmark makes fixed-horizon instability visible. The
stream alternates between slow drift, fast bursts, recovery plateaus, and
micro-bursts with irregular durations, so the local oracle horizon need not be
globally stable. In that setting, a single fixed window can look reasonable on
average while remaining locally misaligned for long stretches. This benchmark
separates three questions that are often conflated: whether the oracle horizon
is time-varying, whether an online regulator can track that changing scale, and
how far each method remains from the local oracle horizon over time.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\safeincludegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/cuberoot_adwin/fig_horizon_instability}
\caption{Alternating-timescales instability. The top panel shows the drift
schedule, the middle panel shows the local oracle horizon alongside fixed
windows and the cube-root regulator, and the bottom panel shows horizon regret
\,$|n_t - n_t^*|\,$ over time. The purpose of the benchmark is to expose when
the useful horizon changes locally: slow stretches favor longer memory, bursts
contract the oracle scale, and fixed horizons can remain misaligned across
regime changes. The figure makes the temporal-validity problem visible
directly.}
\label{fig:horizon_instability}
\end{figure}

This benchmark makes two things visible. First, horizon validity can vary even
when aggregate prediction error stays competitive. Second, the adjustment is
asymmetric: contraction of the useful horizon is easier to trigger than
re-expansion after a low-drift stretch. The pattern is operational evidence of
path dependence in temporal memory adaptation. Proposition~\ref{prop:asymmetric_horizon_recovery}
formalizes the same asymmetry in the idealized EMA-to-horizon map.

\begin{proposition}[Asymmetric Horizon Recovery]\label{prop:asymmetric_horizon_recovery}
Let the drift estimator satisfy the EMA recursion
\[
  \hat\zeta_t \,=\, \alpha\,\Delta_t + q\,\hat\zeta_{t-1},
  \qquad q \coloneqq 1-\alpha \in (0,1),
\]
with $\Delta_t = \delta_{\mathrm{slow}}$ in the slow regime and $\Delta_t = M$
in the burst regime. Assume the horizon map is unclipped on
$[\delta_{\mathrm{slow}}, M]$ so that
\[
  n^*(\delta) \,=\, \left(\frac{C_K}{\delta}\right)^{2/3}
\]
throughout the transition. Write
$n_b \coloneqq n^*(M)$ and $n_s \coloneqq n^*(\delta_{\mathrm{slow}})$.
For any $0 < \varepsilon < n_s - n_b$, define the inverse thresholds
\[
  \delta_b(\varepsilon) \coloneqq C_K\,(n_b + \varepsilon)^{-3/2},
  \qquad
  \delta_s(\varepsilon) \coloneqq C_K\,(n_s - \varepsilon)^{-3/2}.
\]
Then the exact recovery time from the slow regime to the burst regime is
\[
  T_{\mathrm{contract}}(\varepsilon)
  \,=\,
  \left\lceil
  \frac{\log\!\bigl((M-\delta_{\mathrm{slow}})/(M-\delta_b(\varepsilon))\bigr)}{\log(1/q)}
  \right\rceil,
\]
while, after a burst of duration $T_{\mathrm{burst}}$ steps, the exact recovery
time back to the slow regime is
\[
  T_{\mathrm{expand}}(\varepsilon)
  \,=\,
  \left\lceil
  \frac{\log\!\bigl((\hat\zeta_{\mathrm{end}}-\delta_{\mathrm{slow}})/
  (\delta_s(\varepsilon)-\delta_{\mathrm{slow}})\bigr)}{\log(1/q)}
  \right\rceil,
\]
where
\[
  \hat\zeta_{\mathrm{end}} \,=\, M - (M-\delta_{\mathrm{slow}})q^{T_{\mathrm{burst}}}
\]
is the burst-end drift estimate. If
\[
  T_{\mathrm{burst}} \,\geq\, T_0(\varepsilon)
  \;\coloneqq\;
  \left\lceil \frac{\log(1-\rho_\varepsilon)}{\log q} \right\rceil,
  \qquad
  \rho_\varepsilon \coloneqq
  \frac{\delta_s(\varepsilon)-\delta_{\mathrm{slow}}}{M-\delta_b(\varepsilon)},
\]
then $T_{\mathrm{expand}}(\varepsilon) > T_{\mathrm{contract}}(\varepsilon)$.
Moreover, for small $\varepsilon$,
\[
  M-\delta_b(\varepsilon)
  = \tfrac{3}{2} C_K n_b^{-5/2}\,\varepsilon + O(\varepsilon^2),
  \qquad
  \delta_s(\varepsilon)-\delta_{\mathrm{slow}}
  = \tfrac{3}{2} C_K n_s^{-5/2}\,\varepsilon + O(\varepsilon^2),
\]
 so the slow-side threshold distance is smaller by a factor
 $\bigl(\delta_{\mathrm{slow}}/M\bigr)^{5/3}(1+o(1))$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The EMA recursion has the closed form
\[
  \hat\zeta_{t_0+k} = \Delta^* + \bigl(\hat\zeta_{t_0}-\Delta^*\bigr)q^k
\]
whenever the regime is constant with fixed point $\Delta^*$. Hence after the
transition from $\delta_{\mathrm{slow}}$ to $M$,
\[
  \hat\zeta_{t_0+k} = M - (M-\delta_{\mathrm{slow}})q^k,
\]
and after the return to the slow regime,
\[
  \hat\zeta_{t_0+T_{\mathrm{burst}}+k}
  = \delta_{\mathrm{slow}} + (\hat\zeta_{\mathrm{end}}-\delta_{\mathrm{slow}})q^k.
\]

Because $n^*(\delta)$ is monotone decreasing on the unclipped interval and
its inverse is $g(n)=C_K n^{-3/2}$, the condition
$|n^*(\hat\zeta_{t_0+k})-n_b|\leq \varepsilon$ is equivalent to
\(\hat\zeta_{t_0+k} \geq \delta_b(\varepsilon)\), which gives the displayed
formula for $T_{\mathrm{contract}}(\varepsilon)$. Likewise,
$|n^*(\hat\zeta_{t_0+T_{\mathrm{burst}}+k})-n_s|\leq \varepsilon$
is equivalent to
\(\hat\zeta_{t_0+T_{\mathrm{burst}}+k} \leq \delta_s(\varepsilon)\),
which yields $T_{\mathrm{expand}}(\varepsilon)$.

The inequality $T_{\mathrm{expand}}(\varepsilon) > T_{\mathrm{contract}}(\varepsilon)$
holds whenever the burst lasts long enough that
\[
  1-q^{T_{\mathrm{burst}}} \geq \rho_\varepsilon,
\]
since this makes the numerator of $T_{\mathrm{expand}}$ larger than that of
$T_{\mathrm{contract}}$ by the exact threshold relation above. The small-$\varepsilon$
expansions follow by Taylor expansion of $n \mapsto C_K n^{-3/2}$ around
$n_b$ and $n_s$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Effect of clipping]
If clipping at $n_{\min}$ is active near the burst side, then contraction can only
be faster. The observed asymmetry is therefore conservative: clipping weakens the
theoretical barrier in the burst regime, but it cannot eliminate the longer slow-
side recovery.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Drift-EMA Sensitivity}\label{sec:validation_ema_sensitivity}

The recovery asymmetry persists when the drift estimator itself is varied. The
next sweep changes the EMA coefficient $\alpha$ in the drift estimator while
keeping the alternating-timescales benchmark fixed. Larger $\alpha$ makes the
drift estimate more reactive, reducing contraction regret while expansion regret
stays persistently larger; the expansion-to-contraction ratio remains above one
throughout the sweep and is higher at the larger $\alpha$ values.

\subsection{Horizon Cost Curve}\label{sec:validation_gap_cost}

The next question is when horizon misalignment becomes operationally expensive.
For each backend, we compare its instantaneous horizon $n_t$ with the local
cube-root target $n_t^*$ and plot the excess error $E_t - E_t^\star$ against both
the absolute gap $|n_t - n_t^*|$ and the relative gap $|\log(n_t / n_t^*)|$.
Here $E_t^\star$ is the best error achieved at time $t$ by the fixed-window oracle
grid, and the curve is binned over all seeds and time points.

useful-memory scale, together with backend-specific degradation once that band
is exceeded.

\subsection{Phase-Wise Recovery Check}\label{sec:validation_piecewise}

The piecewise-drift benchmark changes the local drift rate in three phases, and
the adaptive horizon is compared with the offline oracle fixed window for each
phase. The calibration is held fixed across phases; only the stream changes.
The detailed calibration check is reported in \cref{app:piecewise_recovery}. In
the main text, the point is simply that one calibration remains in the right
operational range when the drift regime changes discretely.

\subsection{What the Figures Show}\label{sec:validation_summary}

Taken together, the figures support three claims. First, the cube-root law is a
real geometric law for useful memory rather than a fitting artifact. Second,
horizon tracking is distinct from changepoint detection: memory can become stale
before statistical evidence is strong enough to declare a change. Third,
horizon control has operational value, but its predictive payoff depends on the
backend and on the drift regime.
