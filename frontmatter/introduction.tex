\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Validity under drift operates through an information state built from recent data.
A windowed empirical law, a geometric estimator, a conformal calibration set, or
a controller memory all compress the past and are then used to estimate,
calibrate, or decide in the present. These objects are useful only while the
retained information remains valid for the current target.

Write $\phi$ for a pipeline that retains recent data, updates that retained
state, and extracts a present-time readout. Let $\tau=\{\tau_d:d\in\mathcal D\}$
be a flow family indexed by dimensions such as time, space, structure, or
operation. The validity functional
\[
  V(\phi,\tau)
  = \inf_{n\ge 1}\bigl(C_K(\phi)n^{-a(\phi)} + C_S\zeta(\tau)n^{H(\tau)}\bigr)
\]
assigns a persistence value to that pipeline against a flow class $\tau$.
Here $C_K n^{-a}$ is the finite-sample acquisition term induced by the chosen
state geometry, while $C_S\zeta n^H$ is the drift-induced dissipation of that
state under the admissible flow. Retention lowers sampling noise and ages the
retained information at the same time. Detection and calibration expose the same
mechanism: an alarm can remain silent after retained evidence has already become
stale, and a calibration set can lose coverage before any detector reacts.

Let $P_t$ denote the present target law, let $\bar P_t^{(n)}$ denote the window
target formed from the last $n$ target laws, and let $\widehat P_t^{(n)}$ be the
estimator built from the last $n$ observations. In the deterministic
H"older--Wasserstein constitutive model, tracking error separates into a
finite-sample term relative to $\bar P_t^{(n)}$ and a staleness term between
$\bar P_t^{(n)}$ and $P_t$:
\[
  \mathbb E\,d\!\left(\widehat P_t^{(n)},P_t\right)
  \le C_K n^{-a} + C_S\zeta n^H.
\]
When the finite-sample term decays like $n^{-a}$ and the staleness term grows
like $\zeta n^H$, their balance defines the validity horizon and the induced
useful-memory region. In the deterministic H"older--Wasserstein constitutive
model, this balance realizes the validity functional
\[
  V(\phi,\tau)=\inf_{n\ge 1}\bigl(C_K(\phi)n^{-a(\phi)}+C_S\zeta(\tau)n^{H(\tau)}\bigr).
\]

The finite-sample exponent $a$ depends on the geometry used to compare
distributions or evaluate readouts, while the roughness exponent $H$ and
amplitude $\zeta$ describe how quickly retained information loses validity.
The resulting horizon is structural: it is imposed jointly by metric geometry
and drift regularity. The temporal coordinate is one slice of this validity
surface: if $d=\mathrm{time}$ is one coordinate in $\mathcal D$, then
$V(\phi,\tau_d)$ recovers validity as a special case.

The law raises three linked tasks: identify the horizon imposed by sampling and
drift, infer that horizon from data, and act on it when the memory scale becomes
a control variable. The main theorem closes the exponent of the horizon law on a
Gaussian location benchmark over a deterministic H"older class. A tractable
one-dimensional root-$n$ proof anchor and projection-based transfer show that the
same mechanism persists beyond that benchmark.

Horizon inference becomes possible when lag geometry reveals the drift law.
Plug-in asymptotics from lag geometry yield confidence structure for the horizon
and an operational identifiability score for the useful-memory band. Sequential
control closes the operational layer: once update cost is explicit, the best
policy depends on drift roughness and on actuation price, producing a phase map
rather than a universal winner.

The same object also organizes calibration under drift. A conformal calibration
window is retained information used to deliver present-time coverage. In the
split-conformal benchmark reported here, the calibration score exhibits the same
U-curve geometry as distribution tracking: small windows are noisy, large
windows are stale, coverage degrades before alarm, and the safe calibration band
aligns with the useful-memory band. Conformal prediction is therefore one
operational readout of the validity field.
