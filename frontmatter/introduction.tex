\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Retained evidence under drift is simultaneously informative and obsolete. Larger
memory reduces finite-sample noise, but it also increases the age of the
observations used to estimate the present. Finite-memory distribution tracking
therefore has a temporal-validity horizon: a largest memory scale that remains
useful before drift-induced staleness overtakes the remaining sampling gain.

This horizon is governed by the balance law
\[
  \mathbb E\,d\!\left(\widehat P_t^{(n)},P_t\right)
  \leq
  C_K n^{-a} + C_S\zeta n^H,
\]
where $a>0$ is the finite-sample rate exponent of the chosen geometry,
$H\in(0,1]$ is the temporal roughness exponent of the drift path, and $\zeta$
is the roughness amplitude. Balancing the finite-sample term against the
staleness term yields the temporal-validity horizon
\[
  n^*(a,H) \asymp (C_K/\zeta)^{1/(a+H)}.
\]
The same envelope induces a useful-memory region: the memory lengths whose
tracking error stays within a fixed factor of the optimum. Its normalized
geometry depends only on $(a,H)$.

This object is structural, but operational use requires more than the existence
of an optimizer. Memory selection from data depends on whether noisy lagged
transport discrepancies can localize the horizon within the useful-memory
region. That question leads to horizon inference and to an identifiability
criterion for useful-memory selection.

The theorem line closes in a tractable one-dimensional bounded-support fixed-
span triangular-array model. There, the quantile representation of $W_2^2$, a
triangular-array Bahadur expansion, and a design-effect comparison yield a
root-$n$ finite-sample term under explicit compact-support, fixed-span, and
integrated-remainder hypotheses. A structural Gaussian lower bound shows that
the horizon is imposed by drift geometry rather than by a particular estimator,
and the Gaussian location model supplies a matching benchmark on deterministic
H\"older paths. Beyond that proof model, finite projection families and sliced
Wasserstein yield transfer results under uniform projected control, while in
fixed-$\varepsilon$ Sinkhorn geometry one-dimensional inheritance is explicit on
compact support and the embedded low-dimensional extension is reduced to a
conditional theorem problem. Empirically, the same object appears through
interior U-curves, roughness-dependent scaling, real-stream diagnostics, and
validity loss before the first detector alarm.

The contribution is fivefold.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\arabic*)},leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Temporal-validity horizon and useful-memory region.}
  The temporal-validity horizon governs finite-memory distribution tracking
  under drift, together with a useful-memory region whose normalized geometry is
  determined by $(a,H)$.

  \item \textbf{Closed theorem line in a tractable proof model.}
  A one-dimensional root-$n$ triangular-array proof model, the exact
  uniform-window staleness bound, a structural Gaussian lower bound, and a
  Gaussian location benchmark show that the horizon is structural.

  \item \textbf{Transfer and operational geometry.}
  Finite projection families and sliced geometries yield transfer theorems,
  one-dimensional fixed-$\varepsilon$ Sinkhorn inheritance is proved, and the
  embedded low-dimensional Sinkhorn step is isolated as a conditional theorem
  problem.

  \item \textbf{Horizon inference and identifiability.}
  Plug-in horizon inference yields an identifiability criterion for
  useful-memory selection and a quadratic regret law for horizon mislocation.

  \item \textbf{Empirical signatures of temporal validity.}
  Empirical results exhibit interior U-curves, roughness-dependent scaling,
  real-stream finite-memory diagnostics, and pre-detection validity loss.
\end{enumerate}

Memory length under drift is a statistical scale set jointly by finite-sample
geometry and temporal roughness.
