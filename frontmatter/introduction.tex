\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Retained evidence under drift is useful because it reduces sampling noise, and
it is harmful because it describes an earlier state of the system. Any method
that tracks a present distribution from a finite memory window must therefore
balance two errors: a finite-sample term that decreases with the window length,
and a staleness term that increases with age. The central object of this paper
is the temporal-validity horizon, the memory scale at which those two forces
balance.

Let $P_t$ denote the present target law, let $\bar P_t^{(n)}$ denote the window
target formed from the last $n$ target laws, and let $\widehat P_t^{(n)}$ be the
estimator built from the last $n$ observations. The governing upper envelope is
\[
  \mathbb E\,d\!\left(\widehat P_t^{(n)},P_t\right)
  \leq
  C_K n^{-a} + C_S\zeta n^H,
\]
where $a>0$ is the finite-sample rate exponent of the chosen geometry,
$H\in(0,1]$ is the temporal roughness exponent of the drift path, and $\zeta$
is the roughness amplitude. Balancing the finite-sample term against the
staleness term yields the temporal-validity horizon
\[
  n^*(a,H) \asymp (C_K/\zeta)^{1/(a+H)}.
\]
The same envelope induces a useful-memory region: the memory lengths whose
tracking error stays within a fixed factor of the optimum. Its normalized
geometry depends only on $(a,H)$.

This law identifies a statistical scale rather than a tuning heuristic. The
finite-sample exponent $a$ depends on the geometry used to compare
distributions, while the roughness exponent $H$ and amplitude $\zeta$ describe
how quickly retained evidence loses temporal validity. The resulting horizon is
structural: it is imposed jointly by metric geometry and drift regularity, not
by a particular estimator. Operational use then raises a second question: when
can noisy lagged discrepancies identify the horizon well enough to keep a
data-driven memory choice inside the useful-memory region?

The theorem line closes in three theorem-level realizations of the same law.
The first is a tractable one-dimensional bounded-support fixed-span triangular-
array model, where the quantile representation of $W_2^2$, a triangular-array
Bahadur expansion, and a design-effect comparison yield a root-$n$ finite-
sample term under explicit compact-support, fixed-span, and integrated-remainder
hypotheses. The second is bounded-cardinality finite discrete embedded Sinkhorn,
where finite-state minorization and finite-support root-$n$ fluctuations close
the same law. The third is a calibrated support-growth family, where a row-
kernel parity decomposition and local block-curvature control close the same
root-$n$ law. A structural Gaussian lower bound and a Gaussian location
benchmark show that the horizon is imposed by drift geometry rather than by a
particular estimator. Beyond those closed classes, finite projection families
and sliced Wasserstein yield transfer results under uniform projected control.
Empirically, the same object appears through interior U-curves,
roughness-dependent scaling, real-stream diagnostics, and validity loss before
the first detector alarm.

This object sits at the intersection of three neighboring lines of work without
reducing to any of them. Like local stationarity and bandwidth selection, it
balances variance against temporal mismatch. Like transport theory, it depends
sharply on the geometry that supplies the finite-sample exponent. Like
sequential testing, it separates the timescale on which retained evidence stops
representing the present from the timescale on which a detector accumulates
enough evidence to trigger an alarm. The paper is organized around that
statistical object rather than around a single proof technology.

The contribution is fivefold:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\arabic*)},leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Object: temporal-validity horizon and useful-memory region.}
  The temporal-validity horizon governs finite-memory distribution tracking
  under drift, together with a useful-memory region whose normalized geometry is
  determined by $(a,H)$.

  \item \textbf{Theorem line: proof anchor and closed classes.}
  A one-dimensional root-$n$ triangular-array proof anchor, the exact
  uniform-window staleness bound, a structural Gaussian lower bound, a Gaussian
  location benchmark, and theorem-level embedded Sinkhorn closures show that the
  horizon is structural across tractable and operational geometries.

  \item \textbf{Transfer: projection families and Sinkhorn geometry.}
  Finite projection families and sliced geometries yield transfer theorems,
  one-dimensional fixed-$\varepsilon$ Sinkhorn inheritance is proved, and the
  bounded-cardinality finite-discrete and calibrated support-growth embedded
  Sinkhorn classes satisfy theorem-level horizon laws.

  \item \textbf{Inference: identifiability and regret.}
  Plug-in horizon inference yields an identifiability criterion for
  useful-memory selection and a quadratic regret law for horizon mislocation.

  \item \textbf{Empirics: signatures of temporal validity.}
  Empirical results exhibit interior U-curves, roughness-dependent scaling,
  real-stream finite-memory diagnostics, and pre-detection validity loss.
\end{enumerate}

Memory length under drift is a statistical scale set jointly by finite-sample
geometry and temporal roughness.
