\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Retained evidence under drift has two competing effects. Larger memory reduces
finite-sample noise, but it also increases the age of the observations used to
estimate the present. The resulting object is a temporal-validity horizon: the
largest memory scale that remains useful before drift-induced staleness overtakes
the remaining sampling gain.

The horizon law takes the form
\[
  \mathbb E\,d\!\left(\widehat P_t^{(n)},P_t\right)
  \leq
  C_K n^{-a} + C_S\zeta n^H,
\]
where $a>0$ is the finite-sample rate exponent of the chosen geometry and
estimator, $H\in(0,1]$ is the temporal roughness exponent of the drift path,
and $\zeta$ is the roughness amplitude. Balancing these terms yields the
temporal-validity horizon
\[
  n^*(a,H) \asymp (C_K/\zeta)^{1/(a+H)}.
\]
The horizon depends jointly on finite-sample geometry and on the temporal
regularity with which drift accumulates staleness.

At any fixed tolerance $\delta>0$, the same envelope induces a useful-memory
region: the memory lengths whose total tracking error remains within a fixed
factor of the optimum. Its normalized shape depends only on $(a,H)$.

A horizon can exist structurally while still being statistically difficult to
locate from noisy lagged transport discrepancies. The inferable-horizon layer
makes that bridge explicit: lag-geometry information must exceed the curvature
scale of the useful-memory region for data-driven memory selection to be
reliable.

The tractable one-dimensional bounded-support fixed-span triangular-array model
establishes the law through the quantile representation of $W_2^2$, a
triangular-array Bahadur expansion, and a design-effect comparison. In that
regime, the finite-sample term satisfies the root-$n$ rate
\[
  \mathbb E\,W_2\!\left(\widehat P_n^{\mathrm{tri}},\bar P_n\right)
  = O(n^{-1/2}).
\]
under explicit compact-support, fixed-span, and integrated-remainder
hypotheses. This yields a closed theorem line in a tractable one-dimensional
bounded-support fixed-span model.

The structural lower bound shows that the horizon is imposed by the drift
geometry itself. A Gaussian lower-bound construction sharpens the constant-level
geometry, and in the Gaussian location model the horizon yields a minimax
benchmark on deterministic H\"older paths.

For fixed-$\varepsilon$ Sinkhorn geometry on an embedded fixed-span model, the
proved structural ingredients yield one-dimensional inheritance results on
compact support, while the embedded low-dimensional extension isolates the
embedded operational gap.

Broader regular distribution families and operational geometries support
different finite-sample rates, and therefore different members of the horizon
family. The compact-support one-dimensional proof model, the structural Gaussian
lower bound, and the Gaussian location benchmark close the core law, while
finite projection families, sliced Wasserstein, and fixed-$\varepsilon$ Sinkhorn
exhibit transfer or operational versions of the horizon logic.

The temporal-validity horizon and the induced useful-memory region are the
governing structural objects for finite-memory tracking under drift. Temporal
validity and changepoint evidence are distinct statistical clocks: validity can
fail before alarm. The inferable-horizon layer then propagates lag-geometry
uncertainty into plug-in horizon coverage and operational regret.

The finite-memory U-curve, roughness-dependent horizon scaling, the
fixed-$\varepsilon$ Sinkhorn evidence, and the measurable lag between validity
loss and the first detector alarm appear in the experiments. The neighboring
literatures on regret, window adaptation, smoothing, filtering, and transport
geometry isolate related pieces of that story; the analysis here identifies the
time scale on which retained evidence remains temporally valid before temporal age
becomes the dominant source of error.
