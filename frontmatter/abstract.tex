\begin{abstract}
We study the problem of tracking non-stationary environments under finite memory
constraints. When observations are aggregated over time, estimation variance
decreases with window size, but delay-induced bias increases as older data
becomes less representative under drift. We formalize this trade-off and show
that, in the finite-memory setting considered here, it induces a cube-root
scaling law: the minimum achievable tracking error scales as $\zeta^{1/3}$ with
the drift rate $\zeta$, with an optimal effective memory that scales as
$\zeta^{-2/3}$. Empirically, we recover a log-log slope of 0.316 in controlled
experiments, closely matching the theoretical prediction.

We further consider action-coupled systems, where observations are influenced by
the system's own interventions. In this setting, apparent improvements in
tracking performance may arise from increased control effort rather than
improved estimation, leading to a form of feedback-induced masking. To address
this, we introduce an effort-aware coherence diagnostic that separates genuine
adaptation from action-induced alignment. Empirical evaluations on synthetic
trackers and real-world datasets show that standard passive drift detectors
capture distributional change but do not distinguish these regimes under
feedback, while the proposed diagnostic identifies early transitions under
intervention. These results suggest that tracking under drift is fundamentally
constrained by temporal staleness, and that evaluating adaptive systems requires
accounting for the interaction between estimation and control.

\end{abstract}
