\begin{abstract}
Memory is not always an asset under drift. Past data reduce sampling noise, but
as the target distribution moves, retaining more evidence also accumulates
temporal misalignment with the present.
Finite-memory distribution tracking is therefore governed by a
temporal-validity problem: how long can retained evidence improve estimation of
the present before drift-induced misalignment overtakes variance reduction?

We answer this question by separating finite-memory error into the carrier of
the measurement layer and the roughness-controlled staleness of the drift path.
This yields a carrier-roughness useful-memory horizon law,
\[
  \mathbb E\,d\!\left(\widehat P_t^{(n)},P_t\right)
  \leq
  C_K n^{-a} + C_S\zeta n^H,
\]
where $a$ is the carrier exponent and $H\in(0,1]$ is the roughness exponent.
Optimizing this balance gives the useful-memory scale
$n^*(a,H)\asymp (C_K/\zeta)^{1/(a+H)}$ and the corresponding optimized error
law.

We instantiate the law with a rigorous minimum kernel: a one-dimensional
bounded-support fixed-span triangular array in which a quantile/Bahadur argument
gives the canonical $a=1/2$ carrier. A Gaussian witness lower bound shows that
the resulting horizon is structural on this main slice. The same decomposition
also identifies regimes where useful memory persists beyond the minimum kernel:
low intrinsic dimension can preserve the raw $W_2$ carrier, while fixed-
$\varepsilon$ Sinkhorn geometry provides a practical measurement layer with
stable finite-sample carriers.

The resulting viewpoint is adjacent to dynamic regret and adaptive windowing,
but the object is different: temporal validity of retained evidence. Useful
memory has a path-dependent horizon, and retained evidence can become invalid
for the present before changepoint evidence becomes statistically visible.

\end{abstract}
