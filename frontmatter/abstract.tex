\begin{abstract}
Under drift, memory helps and ages at the same time: retaining more past
information reduces variance, but it also increases the age of the evidence used
to represent the present. For finite-memory tracking, the central question is
therefore not only how much to remember, but how long remembered evidence
remains temporally valid.

We study this trade-off under a worst-case local $W_2$-Lipschitz drift
envelope. For the finite-memory averaging class analyzed here, the tracking
error decomposes into a decreasing finite-sample term and an increasing
staleness term. In the induced linear-staleness regime, this yields a
\textbf{cube-root horizon law}: the useful-memory horizon obeys
$\boldsymbol{n^* \asymp (C_K/\zeta)^{2/3}}$, and the minimum tracking error
obeys $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}_{\min} \asymp C_K^{2/3}\zeta^{1/3}}$. In the
critical-window regime, we also prove a minimax lower bound on a Gaussian
location subclass, showing that the resulting finite-memory floor is structural
rather than estimator-specific.

Empirically, Gaussian drift experiments recover the predicted $U$-curve and a
finite useful-memory optimum. Continuous-drift benchmarks then expose the
practical thesis of the paper: temporal validity is not changepoint evidence.
They reveal a cap-only regime in which retained memory is already stale while a
detector remains statistically silent. Alternating-timescales experiments show
that contraction is easier than re-expansion, and horizon-misalignment
experiments show that stale memory can carry a nonlinear predictive cost.

The operational consequence is conservative horizon control: a horizon cap can
remove stale memory before a detector has enough evidence to alarm.

\end{abstract}
