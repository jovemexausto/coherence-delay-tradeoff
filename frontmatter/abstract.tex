\begin{abstract}
Under drift, memory helps and ages at the same time: retaining more past
information reduces variance, but it also increases the age of the evidence used
to represent the present. The central question is therefore not only how much to
remember, but how long remembered evidence remains temporally valid.

We study the temporal validity of finite memory under drift and show that
useful memory has a finite horizon determined by temporal roughness. For path
classes in which drift-induced staleness accumulates as $\zeta n^H$, the
variance-staleness balance yields roughness-indexed horizon laws
$n_H^* \asymp (C_K/\zeta)^{2/(1+2H)}$ and the corresponding minimax rates. The
worst-case $W_2$-Lipschitz envelope is the $H=1$ anchor, recovering the
cube-root horizon $n^* \asymp (C_K/\zeta)^{2/3}$. A Gaussian location lower
bound shows that the same scaling defines a structural finite-memory floor.

This also reveals a two-clock structure in drifting systems: retained evidence
can lose temporal validity before a changepoint becomes statistically
detectable. In finite samples, horizon sweeps exhibit a structural $U$-curve
with a finite optimum, and that optimum shifts with temporal roughness across
path classes. The same separation between validity expiry and statistical
detection appears as a positive invalidity gap, while departures from the
useful-memory scale incur measurable excess error.

\end{abstract}
