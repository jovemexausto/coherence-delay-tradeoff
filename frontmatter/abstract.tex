\begin{abstract}
Additional memory reduces finite-sample noise but also increases the age of the
evidence used to estimate the present. Finite-memory distribution tracking is
therefore governed by a temporal-validity horizon. A general horizon law
separates finite-memory tracking error into a finite-sample term, determined by
the chosen geometry and estimator, and a drift-induced staleness term,
determined by the regularity of the target path:
\[
  \mathbb E\,d\!\left(\widehat P_t^{(n)},P_t\right)
  \leq
  C_K n^{-a} + C_S\zeta n^H.
\]
Here $a>0$ is the finite-sample rate exponent and $H\in(0,1]$ is the temporal
roughness exponent. Balancing these terms yields the temporal-validity horizon
$n^*(a,H)\asymp (C_K/\zeta)^{1/(a+H)}$.

We call a memory length useful when retained evidence remains temporally valid,
so that the balance above still governs the tracking error. The corresponding
useful-memory scale is $n^*(a,H)$.

A tractable one-dimensional bounded-support fixed-span model establishes the
root-$n$ regime, and a Gaussian lower bound shows that the resulting horizon is
structural rather than estimator-specific. In the Gaussian location class this
yields a minimax benchmark on deterministic H\"older paths, while in
fixed-$\varepsilon$ Sinkhorn geometry the same balance is theorem-level on
compact one-dimensional support and remains conjectural for broader embedded
bandwise inheritance.

Memory under drift is governed by temporal validity, not by a universal window
rule. The horizon depends jointly on finite-sample geometry and temporal
roughness, and retained evidence can become invalid for the present before
change becomes statistically detectable.

\end{abstract}
