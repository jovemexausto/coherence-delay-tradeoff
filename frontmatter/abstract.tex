\begin{abstract}
We investigate the fundamental limits of tracking an evolving distribution under
temporal drift. At the core of this problem lies a critical trade-off: longer memory
reduces finite-sample estimation noise but introduces staleness due to an increasing
age mismatch with the present state. This balance is formalized by defining a finite
temporal-validity horizon and demonstrating that the optimal memory scale satisfies
$n^*(a,H) \asymp (C_K/\zeta)^{1/(a+H)}$, where the finite-sample rate exponent $a$ and
the temporal roughness exponent $H$ govern the expansion and contraction of this validity
horizon. These theoretical limits are then established within a tractable one-dimensional framework,
leveraging a Gaussian lower bound to prove that this boundary is structural rather than
estimator-specific. Finally, empirical evidence confirms the predicted interior memory
optima and roughness-dependent scaling. Notably, our results reveal a measurable lag
between the loss of temporal validity and the activation of standard changepoint alarms,
showing that under drift, retained evidence can cease to represent the present long before
a distribution change becomes statistically detectable.
\end{abstract}
