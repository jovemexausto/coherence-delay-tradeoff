\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Drifted optimization and regret.}
Dynamic-regret and variation-budget analyses show that under drift, forgetting
scales and window lengths must adapt to how quickly the target moves
\citep{BesbesGurZeevi2015,CheungSimchiLeviZhu2019,YuanLamperski2020,
MazzettoUpfal2023,WangXieChenLuiZhou2024}. The temporal-validity functional asks
how long retained evidence remains valid before age becomes the dominant source
of tracking error.

Singular learning theory provides static equations of state for inference in
nonregular models \citep{Watanabe2009}. The temporal-validity
functional plays the dynamic role: it couples finite-sample geometry and drift
regularity into a single persistence law under nonstationarity.

The same structural trade-off also appears in drifting density estimation.
\citet{MazzettoUpfal2023} derive minimax rates by optimizing over recent-memory
windows under bounded drift, which places the present horizon law in direct
contact with finite-memory risk optimization under nonstationarity.

\paragraph{Adaptive windows and detection.}
Adaptive-window and concept-drift methods study when accumulated evidence is
sufficient to react \citep{BifetGavalda2007,Gama2004,GamaSurvey2014}. Those works
provide the reaction timescale. The horizon law adds the validity timescale:
retained evidence can cease to represent the present before change statistics
cross an alarm threshold.

\paragraph{Sequential nonparametric testing.}
Sequential two-sample testing and kernel change detection quantify the evidence

identifies the tracking timescale on which retained evidence ceases to represent
the present, so the two clocks need not agree. Exponentially weighted MMD

The closest stability-only perspective is the nonparametric stability principle
of \citet{HuangWang2023}, which organizes learning under nonstationarity without
an explicit constitutive law in the exponents $(a,H)$. The temporal-validity
functional makes that geometry explicit through a closed-form persistence surface.

Resource-aware scheduling under drift, including RCCDA \citep{PiasecznyEtAl2025RCCDA}, and memory hedging across
multiple forgetting scales, such as MELO-style horizon grids, address a related
operational question: when should adaptation consume budget on an updated memory
scale rather than on a fixed one? The temporal-validity horizon plays the
complementary role of a constitutive prior for that choice. It predicts the
memory scale that a budget-aware controller should target once the effective
roughness parameters are known. Variation budgets and path-length quantities are
alternate summaries of the same nonstationarity resource, and translating them
to $(a,H)$ remains a natural bridge problem.

\paragraph{Filtering and local smoothing.}
Classical adaptive filtering and state-space tracking tune forgetting factors,
gain schedules, or process-noise levels to balance smoothing against
responsiveness \citep{Kalman1960,Jazwinski1970}. Locally stationary smoothing
and bandwidth selection, together with dynamic-regret analyses for time-varying
statistical estimation, solve a closely related trade-off for evolving regression
or spectral targets \citep{Dahlhaus1997,Vogt2012}. The temporal-validity horizon
places that trade-off inside a persistence field for retained evidence, where
the finite-sample exponent depends on the chosen probability geometry and the
resulting balance defines a measurable validity geometry.

\paragraph{Transport geometry.}
Wasserstein distance and Sinkhorn-type estimators provide the finite-sample
geometries that enter the horizon law \citep{FournierGuillin2015,
BoissardLeGouic2014,NilesWeedBach2019,GenevayEtAl2019,MenaWeed2019,
delBarrioEtAl2023,GoldfeldEtAl2022}. Sliced and projection-robust variants
show that geometry choice can change the finite-sample exponent, while intrinsic
dimension and regularization calibration determine which regime is available
\citep{KolouriRohdeHoffmann2018,DeshpandeEtAl2019,PatyCuturi2019,
LinEtAl2020,NadjahiEtAl2020,NietertEtAl2022,GenansWintenberger2026,FaccoEtAl2017}.
Those geometries sit inside a common temporal-validity law. In that law,
dimensionality matters only through the effective finite-sample exponent: raw
multivariate Wasserstein can force slow rates, while projection,
intrinsic-dimension, or regularization mechanisms can preserve a nondegenerate
horizon when they supply a usable exponent. The proved results cover finite-
projection and sliced transfer together with embedded Sinkhorn classes; the
broader high-dimensional message is an effective-exponent interpretation, not a
claim that classical smoothing is the governing ontology.

\paragraph{Age and drift.}
Wasserstein nonstationarity measures and Age-of-Information formulations make
age into an operational variable \citep{JiangLiZhang2020,
KeehanAndersonWiesemann2025,AminiVinas2026,KaulYatesGruteser2012,
YatesEtAl2021AoI}. The temporal-validity horizon gives that variable a
distributional interpretation: age is costly because it accumulates geometric
mismatch with the present.

Recent non-equilibrium accounts of continual learning describe stability through
metastable escape across energy barriers \citep{Kim2026}. The temporal-validity
horizon is the inference-side analogue: it identifies the critical persistence
scale at which retained structure ceases to track the present.
