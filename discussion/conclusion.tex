\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

How long can memory remain useful when the target distribution keeps moving?
Under the drift class studied here, useful memory has a finite temporal horizon,
and in the worst-case linear-staleness regime that horizon obeys a cube-root
law. The governing object is a variance-staleness trade-off: preserving
temporally valid evidence requires limiting the lag induced by old memory.

The theoretical contribution is a decomposition of tracking error into a
variance term and a staleness term, together with a lower bound showing that the
resulting finite-memory floor is structural. The operational contribution is a
horizon regulator: UMR turns the worst-case law into a temporal-validity cap,
which keeps the effective horizon from drifting far into stale-memory territory.

The strongest empirical lesson is that temporal validity and changepoint
evidence are distinct objects. The signature regime is \emph{cap-only}: the
memory horizon becomes operationally stale before there is enough statistical
evidence for a changepoint alarm. Horizon misalignment carries a nonlinear
operational cost, but the transfer from gap to predictive error depends on the
backend and on the drift regime. The alternating-timescales benchmark adds a
secondary lesson: contraction is easier to induce than re-expansion, so
EMA-driven horizon regulators have a recoverability gap.

The real-stream evidence is intentionally secondary. ELEC2, with a short Bikes
summary in the appendix, shows that the temporal-validity gap is not
synthetic-only, but the main claim of the paper remains worst-case and
structural.

Finite-memory systems are best judged by how long their retained evidence
remains current enough to matter. The design consequence is simple: treat memory
as a resource with a finite horizon, separate temporal validity from changepoint
evidence, and use conservative horizon regulation to avoid stale-memory carryover.

The cube-root exponent is tied to the worst-case linear-staleness envelope
studied here, and the lower bound is established through a Gaussian location
subclass. The next step is correspondingly disciplined: characterize narrower
path classes and better local drift estimators without diluting the current
worst-case statement.
